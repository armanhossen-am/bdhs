Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) provide much of what is known about fertility, family planning, maternal and child health and nutrition in low- and middle-income countries. Bangladesh has taken part since the early 1990s and has now completed nine rounds: 1993-94, 1996-97, 1999-2000, 2004, 2007, 2011, 2014, 2017-18 and 2022. The surveys are carried out by the National Institute of Population Research and Training (NIPORT) with technical assistance from ICF [1]. Official estimates appear in the final reports and through the DHS Program’s online tools, while the respondent-level files are released to registered researchers.

These files support a large secondary literature, yet published estimates from them are seldom compared with the official figures. When they differ, readers cannot tell whether the difference is substantive or simply a computational slip. The definitions in the Guide to DHS Statistics [2] are clear, but applying them to Bangladesh requires attention to details that are not visible in the data files. The women’s samples are restricted to women who have ever been married, so indicators for all women need adjustment factors. Division boundaries and codes changed three times between rounds. The 2022 survey gave a third of women a shortened questionnaire. Some questions are specific to Bangladesh and do not belong in the standard definitions. An error at any of these points carries through to the published result without warning.

Several tools already help with parts of this work. The rdhs package gives access to the DHS Program’s data and indicator services [3], DHS.rates computes fertility and childhood mortality rates [4], the survey package implements design-based estimation [5], and IPUMS-DHS distributes harmonised extracts of selected variables across countries [6]. What is missing is a way to check an analysis of the original files against the official estimates, a record of the country-specific decisions this requires, and a way to share reproducible analyses when the data themselves cannot be shared.

This paper introduces bdhs, a package for R [7] that covers all BDHS rounds and uses it to answer four questions. How closely can the official estimates be reproduced from the recode files? How much do common analytic errors change the results? What does a systematic data-quality assessment show across rounds? And can synthetic data reproduce the published estimates closely enough to be useful for teaching and for testing analysis code? We illustrate the trend, inequality and decomposition tools with the rise of caesarean delivery between 2004 and 2022, and the bundled official estimates with an assessment of progress towards selected Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) targets.
