This study shows that the official BDHS estimates can be reproduced from the recode files almost exactly, as long as a handful of decisions are made correctly. Once the survey design, denominators, reference periods and round-specific rules were written down in code, 353 of 355 national estimates matched the published values, most of them to within a few hundredths of a percentage point. Inequality measures computed from the microdata also agreed with those derived from the official tables. The same exercise showed that the decisions that matter most are ones the data files do not reveal.

The largest errors come from how the sample was drawn, not from the statistical method. Because every BDHS women’s sample is limited to women who have ever been married, child marriage estimated without all-woman factors comes out about ten percentage points too high. Bangladesh’s child-marriage problem is serious enough without overstating it. In 2022 the error runs the other way: counting women who were never asked about family planning as having no unmet need understates it by a third and would suggest much faster progress since 2017-18 than actually took place.

Other conventions matter less but are still worth getting right. Some are documented only implicitly, in the way the official tables were produced. The child-marriage tables use the total all-woman factor for every subgroup, while the subgroup factors are used elsewhere. The exclusive breastfeeding indicator leaves out the country-specific liquids that the questionnaire records. Neither choice can be inferred from the data documentation alone, and each moves estimates by more than their sampling error.

Design effects of 1.6 to 3.1 are typical of DHS surveys but are still often ignored. Treating the BDHS as a simple random sample makes estimates look more precise than they are, which matters most when comparing subgroups or rounds. Regional comparisons over time raise a further, practical problem: because divisions were renumbered when Mymensingh division was created, the same code refers to different places in different rounds. Any regional trend analysis needs harmonised division boundaries.

The data-quality assessment is mostly reassuring. Birth histories are complete, and there is no sign of the displacement of births out of the health-question window that distorts estimates in some DHS countries. Anthropometric measurement improved markedly after 2011, which supports the comparability of recent nutrition estimates. Three patterns deserve attention, and all are consistent with earlier assessments of age and date reporting in DHS surveys [8]:

- height-for-age z-scores remain more dispersed than expected, which may slightly inflate stunting estimates;
- the heaping of deaths at 12 months moves some deaths between the infant and child mortality intervals;
- the shortage of women recorded at age 50 relative to 49 points to age misreporting at the edge of eligibility for the women’s interview.

The calibrated synthetic data meets a practical need. Because DHS files cannot be shared, the analysis code in published papers usually cannot be run by readers, students practice on toy data, and new methods are hard to demonstrate. Synthetic files that reproduce the published national and regional estimates let worked examples behave like the real survey without containing information about any respondent. They suit teaching, software testing and reproducible demonstrations. They should not be used for substantive conclusions, because the associations between indicators are not reproduced.

The case study shows how the tools work together. Caesarean delivery in Bangladesh rose roughly ten-fold in less than two decades, part of a rapid global increase [21]. As its use spread beyond the richest households, absolute inequality widened while relative inequality fell. Changes in maternal education and household wealth explain only a small part of the rise which occurred within every group. That pattern points to changes in health-system practice rather than in the characteristics of the mothers, and is consistent with our earlier finding that caesarean delivery has spread through the private sector and become less tied to medical indications [20]. Separating these components with proper standard errors is straightforward once the design and indicator definitions are right.

bdhs complements existing tools rather than replacing them. IPUMS-DHS remains the better choice for comparisons between countries with harmonised variables [6], and rdhs and DHS.rates serve every DHS country [3, 4]. bdhs reproduces DHS.rates exactly for the rates both packages compute. Its contribution is depth for one country: every round, the original files, validation against the official estimates, and Bangladesh-specific harmonisation and documentation.

The study has limitations. Validation was limited to the recode files available to the authors, mainly the children’s records for the earlier rounds, with complete sets only for 2017-18 and 2022. No files from 1996-97 were available, and its harmonisation rules, taken from the published codebook, remain unverified. A tolerance of half a percentage point for national estimates does not guarantee agreement for every subgroup, although the subgroup comparisons we made agreed closely. The synthetic data reproduces only the calibrated values. Finally, the package does not yet produce estimates below the division level, which are increasingly needed for planning.
